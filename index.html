      How tornadoes form (6 steps)

      These match the six numbered stages in the animation above.

      1. ☀️ Heating. The sun warms the ground, which warms the moist air touching it. Warm air is lighter than the cooler air above, so it starts to rise — like a hot-air balloon.
      2. ☁️ Clouds grow. As the air rises it cools, and its water vapor condenses into cloud. Condensing releases heat, which keeps the air warmer than its surroundings, so it rises even faster. That extra push is what CAPE (“fuel”) measures.
      3. 🌀 A spinning roll. When the wind blows faster (or from a different direction) higher up than near the ground, the air in between gets rolled into an invisible horizontal tube, like a pencil rolled between your palms. That's wind shear.
      4. ⬆️ The roll stands up. A strong updraft lifts the middle of that tube and tips it upright. Now the updraft itself is spinning — a mesocyclone — and the storm is a supercell. Its top spreads out into a flat anvil.
      5. ⬇️ Wall cloud and downdraft. Under the rotation, a lower, often spinning “wall cloud” forms. Behind it, rain-cooled air sinks and wraps around the rotation (the rear-flank downdraft).
      6. 🌪️ Touchdown. The downdraft and the strong low-level spin tighten the rotation near the ground, like a figure skater pulling in their arms. If it becomes intense and connects cloud to ground, it's a tornado.
        Simplified: scientists still don't fully know why some supercells make tornadoes and most don't.
      Glossary
      CAPE
      Convective Available Potential Energy, in J/kg — how much “fuel” a rising bubble of air has. More CAPE = stronger updrafts.
      Dew point
      The temperature air would have to cool to before its moisture condenses. A higher dew point means more water vapor in the air — Oklahoma spring days in the upper 60s °F feel sticky.
      Wind shear
      A change in wind speed or direction with height. It's what makes storms rotate, and it tilts the storm so rain doesn't fall back into the updraft.
      Supercell
      A thunderstorm with a long-lasting rotating updraft. Supercells make most strong tornadoes — but most supercells never make one.
      Mesocyclone
      The rotating updraft inside a supercell, usually 2–10 km (1–6 miles) wide. Much bigger and slower-spinning than a tornado.
      Wall cloud
      A lowered, often rotating section of cloud under a supercell's updraft. Tornadoes usually come from here.
      Rear-flank downdraft (RFD)
      A surge of sinking air on the back side of a supercell that wraps around the mesocyclone. It often helps a tornado form.
      EF scale
      The Enhanced Fujita scale, EF0 to EF5. It rates a tornado after it happens, by estimating wind speed from the damage it left.
      Tornado Alley
      The central U.S. plains, including Oklahoma. Warm, moist air from the Gulf of Mexico meets dry air from the west and fast winds aloft there, so the tornado ingredients come together more often than almost anywhere else on Earth.

      About 3 in 4 U.S. tornadoes are EF0–EF1. EF4–EF5 are rare (around 1 in 100), but they cause most tornado deaths. Moore, Oklahoma has been hit by top-rated tornadoes twice: an F5 in 1999 (rated on the older Fujita scale) and an EF5 in 2013.
